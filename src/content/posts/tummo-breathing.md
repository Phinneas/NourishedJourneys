<p>Tummo breathing is an ancient Tibetan Buddhist practice that literally means "inner fire." Practitioners use forceful breathing and visualization to generate body heat, allowing them to meditate comfortably in freezing conditions wearing only light clothing.</p><p>Modern research confirms that tummo breathing raises core body temperature, increases metabolism, and activates brown fat. It has also been popularized by Wim Hof, though his method differs in some ways from traditional tummo.</p><h2 id="how-to-practice-tummo-breathing">How to Practice Tummo Breathing</h2><p>The traditional practice has two phases: forceful breathing and breath retention.</p><h3 id="phase-1-forceful-breathing">Phase 1: Forceful Breathing</h3><ul><li><strong>Step 1:</strong> Sit comfortably with your spine straight. Place your hands on your belly.</li><li><strong>Step 2:</strong> Inhale deeply and quickly through your nose, expanding your belly.</li><li><strong>Step 3:</strong> Exhale forcefully through your mouth or nose, contracting your belly.</li><li><strong>Step 4:</strong> Continue this rapid, rhythmic breathing for 30 breaths. The pace should be about 1 breath per second.</li></ul><h3 id="phase-2-breath-retention">Phase 2: Breath Retention</h3><ul><li><strong>Step 5:</strong> After the 30th exhale, exhale completely and hold your breath with empty lungs.</li><li><strong>Step 6:</strong> While holding, visualize a flame at the base of your spine growing warmer and rising upward.</li><li><strong>Step 7:</strong> Hold as long as comfortable, then inhale deeply and hold for 10-15 seconds.</li><li><strong>Step 8:</strong> Exhale and repeat the cycle 2-3 times.</li></ul><h2 id="safety-guidelines">Safety Guidelines</h2><p>Tummo breathing is intense. Follow these rules:</p><ul><li>Do not practice near water or while driving.</li><li>Stop if you feel dizzy, nauseous, or chest pain.</li><li>Avoid if you have epilepsy, high blood pressure, or cardiovascular conditions.</li><li>Never practice while pregnant.</li><li>Start with 1-2 rounds and build up gradually.</li></ul><h2 id="what-the-science-shows">What the Science Shows</h2><p>Harvard Medical School researchers studied tummo practitioners and found they could raise their skin temperature by up to 17 degrees Fahrenheit. Brain scans showed increased activity in the insula and hypothalamus, regions associated with body temperature regulation and interoception.</p><h2 id="expected-effects">Expected Effects</h2><p>After practice, you may feel warm, energized, and mentally alert. Some people experience tingling or lightheadedness. These effects are normal and pass within minutes.</p>
